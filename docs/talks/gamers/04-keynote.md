# The Keynote — for gamers (30–40 minutes)

**Title:** *One Game, Every Table: building a haunted house that follows you
anywhere*

**Audience:** a convention or community keynote crowd — tabletop players,
horror-game fans, aspiring campaign writers. No technical background
assumed, and none used. The talk is a story with demonstrations, not a
lecture.
**Format:** 7 acts, ~34 minutes of talk + 5 for questions. Each act lists
its visual, its script beats, and what to cut under time pressure. Timing
checkpoints at the end.

---

## Act I — Two game nights (4 min)

*Visual: split screen — a candle-lit table with dice and minis; a laptop
with a lobby screen.*

Open with the tension every hybrid gamer knows. Tabletop night is the real
thing: friends around a table, dice you can feel, a Game Master doing the
voices. But it needs everyone in one room, one free evening, and someone
who knows the rules cold. Digital night is effortless — but the app plays
an edge case differently than the rulebook, the tutorial taught you a
simplified fake, your friend's console port is its own thing, and somewhere
in there the *game* stopped being one game.

Name the dream: what if there were exactly **one** game — one set of rules,
one memory, one referee — and the browser, the desktop app, the online
table, and a real physical board were all just *windows onto it*? That's
Wicked Ways. The rest of this talk is that promise, kept: the game, the
referee, the dice, the board, and the mods.

## Act II — The house wants all three (6 min)

*Visual: the Health / Sanity / Energy triangle; then the affliction band:
KO · Panic · Fear · Confused.*

Tour the game properly — this act earns the crowd.

**The premise.** A party and a Game Master explore a haunted house room by
room, on turns. Each character has a handful of actions per turn; spend
them and the house takes its turn back.

**Three stats, guarding each other in a circle.** Sanity absorbs Health
damage; Energy absorbs Sanity damage; Health absorbs Energy damage. A
rested guard shrugs hits off entirely; a drained one *doubles* them. The
house doesn't need to kill you — it needs to wear down the guard, then
push. That's a horror pacing engine hiding inside a stat sheet.

**Afflictions stick.** Zero Health: knocked out. Zero Sanity: Panic — you
can barely do anything but flee. Low Sanity: Fear — you can do everything
*except* flee. Zero Energy: Confusion — every action becomes a coin flip.
Each turn you get a shake-off roll that grows more generous the longer
you've suffered. The spiral is mechanized, and it's fair.

**Darkness is a decision.** Dark rooms hide their contents — you can't
attack, loot, or search what you can't see — until someone brings light.
But light-averse monsters see fine in the dark and *burn* in the light:
lighting the room lets you aim and hurts them. Someone has to carry the
lantern. Someone has to decide who.

**And the shipped campaign uses all of it.** Hollow House opens with the
front door thudding shut and a trembling caretaker pressing the cellar key
into your hand before he vanishes. The cellar has things in it that prefer
the dark. The way out is up in the attic, behind an iron key, behind worse.
It's a full campaign: keyed doors, an NPC you can actually converse with, a
dread that gnaws at whoever walks unlit, a doom clock, three different
endings.

## Act III — The referee that never sleeps (5 min)

*Visual: one glowing "brain" with windows around it — browser, desktop,
phone, board — each showing the same room from the same moment.*

Now the promise from Act I, made concrete. Every version of the game — every
*window* — talks to one rules brain. Your move goes in; the brain resolves
it; every window is told what happened. There is no second copy of the
rules anywhere. What that buys, in player terms:

- **It cannot play differently on different tables.** There is no "app
  version" of a rule. There's the rule.
- **Nobody can cheat.** No host advantage, no edited client, no "my screen
  said I hit." Every move from every player goes through the same referee.
  Your screen doesn't decide anything — it just *shows* you things.
- **Saves are perfect.** The game's whole state — mid-turn, mid-panic,
  who's carrying the lantern, which doors are unlocked — reloads exactly.
- **Every session is replayable.** The game keeps a ledger of every move
  and every roll. Replay it and you get the identical session, to the last
  die. Settles rules arguments; also means when something weird happens,
  you can *show* us, exactly.

Beat to land: solo play and multiplayer aren't different games here. Same
brain, same referee — the only difference is how many windows are open.

## Act IV — Dice night (5 min)

*Visual: a real d20 mid-air over the board; the outcome band: 1 stumble ·
2–5 miss · 6–19 hit · 20 crit ×1.5.*

The crowd-pleaser act. Every attack — player or monster — opens with a d20
to-hit. Natural 20: critical, half again the damage. Natural 1: you
*stumble* and hurt yourself. 2–5: clean miss. What lands is then filtered
through armor and the stat triangle.

And at a physical table, **the dice are yours.** Roll your actual d20 for
your attack — or roll for the monster, if the table likes its fate
hands-on — and the game accepts your number. Rather not? "Roll for me,"
and the house rolls.

Here's the part to say slowly, because it's the trick: your real roll
doesn't break the game's perfect memory. The number you rolled is written
into the same ledger as everything else — so the session *with your
physical dice in it* is still exactly replayable. Your dice don't bypass
the game. They *join* it. Superstition fully supported; bookkeeping fully
automatic.

## Act V — The board is a puppet (5 min)

*Visual: the physical board exploded — e-ink tiles, recognized pieces,
player dashboards, dice tray — with strings running up to the "brain."*

The physical board, piece by piece:

- **E-ink tiles** repaint as the party explores. Fog-of-war for real:
  unexplored rooms simply aren't drawn yet. The house reveals itself on
  the table as you dare further in.
- **Pieces the board recognizes.** Set your character's piece down on a
  tile and the board knows — same tap-tech as a contactless card. Moving
  your piece *is* entering your move.
- **Player dashboards** show each character's three stats and their gear.
- **The dice tray** from Act IV — roll your own, or let the house roll.

The design idea to hand the audience: **the board is a puppet.** It has no
rules in it at all — it paints what the brain says and reports what your
hands do. Which is why the *same* board already runs as an on-screen
simulator inside the regular game — same puppet, drawn instead of built —
and why the hardware can be developed and tested end-to-end before a
single tile is manufactured. The board you'll eventually put on your table
is running the exact game you played in your browser last night.

## Act VI — Make your own nightmares (5 min)

*Visual: a campaign file opened like a grimoire — a door line, a dialogue
line, a doom-clock line, each with an arrow into the rendered world.*

The creator act. A campaign is a **text file you can read** — rooms and
their descriptions, doors and their keys, monsters and their habits, loot,
NPC dialogue (with forgiving matching — players can ask about "the cellar"
however they phrase it), victory conditions, and house-wide rules: a doom
clock that ticks every round, a dread that gnaws at whoever walks without
a lantern, a storyteller that narrates as you go.

Say the three promises plainly, because each one usually gets applause:

1. **We have no secret tools.** Hollow House — the whole shipped campaign,
   every door, every ending, the caretaker himself — is written in exactly
   the format your campaign would be. Whatever we can make the game do,
   you can.
2. **A broken mod can't hurt you.** A campaign with an error refuses to
   load, with a message — it never gets far enough to corrupt a save. And
   a *hostile* mod can't cheat either: campaigns ask the game to do
   things from a fixed menu; they can't reach in and rewrite your
   character. The worst a bad mod can be is boring.
3. **Write once, play everywhere.** Your campaign runs in the browser, on
   desktop, online, and on the physical board, automatically. You do
   nothing extra.

Then the ambition and the honesty. The ambition: **as moddable as DOOM** —
the game whose community has kept it alive for thirty years, because the
game and its content were truly separate things. That separation is built
here, from day one, and we prove it by shipping our own campaign through
it. The honesty: the *sharing* part — hand a friend one file, they load it
that evening — is still being finished. The format is done; the loading
dock isn't. Creators in the room: the wall you'd build on is already
standing.

## Act VII — Every table there will ever be (4 min)

*Visual: the brain at center; today's windows solid (browser, desktop,
online, board); tomorrow's ghosted (VR headset, smart speaker, chat
bubble, spectator screen).*

Close by handing the audience the pattern, because by now they can apply
it themselves. Anything that can *show you the house* and *take your
command* can be a table:

- **VR or AR** — the house around you, or ghost-light rising off the
  physical tiles through your phone.
- **Voice** — it's a turn-based game of typed commands at heart; a smart
  speaker is that game with ears. "You hear scratching at the cellar
  door" was born for it.
- **A Discord seat** — a bot holds a chair for your group's missing
  player: commands in, narration out.
- **Spectator tables** — streams and tournament screens that watch the
  ledger live without holding a seat.

None of these would change the game — they'd be new windows on the same
brain, like every window before them. One game. Every table. Bring your
own dice.

## Q&A preparation

Likely questions from a gamer crowd, with honest answers:

- **"When can I buy the board?"** The game side is finished and tested —
  the board already runs as an on-screen simulator, and the control
  hardware runs end-to-end in testing without a physical board attached.
  The tiles-and-pieces hardware itself is in prototyping; no date to
  announce.
- **"How many players?"** A party plus a Game Master; solo play works too
  — same game, the house handles the monsters' turns.
- **"Do I need to know programming to write a campaign?"** No. It's a
  structured text file — if you can write a forum post carefully, you can
  write a campaign. The fancy stuff (doom clocks, dialogue) is more of
  the same file.
- **"Can mods steal my data / break my game?"** A campaign can only ask
  the game to do things from a fixed menu — it can't reach your files,
  your save, or your character sheet directly. Broken ones refuse to
  load.
- **"Is there a phone version?"** The browser version runs on phones
  today; a native app would be another window — same brain.
- **"Why horror?"** The three-stat spiral — wear down the guard, then
  push — *is* horror pacing as a mechanic. But the engine underneath
  doesn't care about genre; campaigns choose the mood.

## Timing checkpoints

- End of Act II: ~10 min. Long? Trim the darkness beat — keep the
  caretaker; the crowd remembers characters, not mechanics.
- End of Act IV: ~20 min. Act IV is the crowd-pleaser; never cut the
  "your dice join the game" beat.
- End of Act VI: ~30 min. Act VI's three promises are the applause lines
  for creators; if desperate, compress the Hollow House inventory in Act
  II instead.
- Act VII lands at ~34 min, leaving 5 for questions in a 40-minute slot.
  For a strict 30: halve Act II's tour, compress Act V to the puppet
  metaphor, and let Act VII be the single "anything can be a table"
  sentence plus the close.
